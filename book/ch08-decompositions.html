Chapter 8

Decompositions: exp, sin, integer math

A Tensor library offers dozens of math functions. A GPU has a few dozen instructions. In between, tinygrad rewrites every method into a handful of transcendental primitives plus ordinary arithmetic, and late in codegen it asks the renderer which primitives it can spell natively. Whatever it cannot spell is expanded into polynomials, bit tricks and selects built from UOps. This chapter follows exp, sin and a // 7 + a % 10 through that machinery to the SASS that ran on the L4, and shows why software exp costs nothing here while software sin triples the run time.

Two layers of decomposition

Decomposition happens twice, at opposite ends of the pipeline, for different reasons.

In the frontend, Tensor methods are written in terms of a small set of ops. Tensor.exp does not create an EXP op; there is none. It multiplies by 1/ln 2 and calls exp2 . log is log2(x)·ln 2 , cos is sin(π/2 − x) , sigmoid is 1/(1 + 2^(−x/ln 2)) , and tanh is 2·sigmoid(2x) − 1 . After this layer, the only transcendental UOps left are EXP2, LOG2, SIN, SQRT and RECIPROCAL, plus one more: Tensor.pow emits Ops.POW , which has its own derivative . Autograd only needs derivatives for those six. POW never reaches a renderer: in codegen the symbolic rewriter turns integer and half-integer constant exponents into multiplies (and a sqrt) and everything else into EXP2/LOG2 with xpow , so every backend only needs to implement the five.

In codegen, after the optimizer has finished and the kernel has been devectorized, the early decompositions and late decompositions passes ask the renderer what it supports. The question is literal: the supported ops are the keys of the renderer's code_for_op dictionary . Any op not in that tuple gets a pattern that rewrites it into ops that are.

Note how much of gelu is gone in the real kernel. The formula in the source is 0.5·x·(1 + tanh(√(2/π)·(x + 0.044715·x³))) . tanh expands to 2σ − 1, and the symbolic rewriter folds 0.5·(1 + (2σ − 1)) down to σ. x**3 is a constant integer power, which simplify_pow turns into x·x·x before the general xpow path can see it. The SASS shows 12 MUFU instructions for 4 elements. Four are MUFU.EX2, one per exp2. The other eight are MUFU.RCP: 1/x in CUDA C is a correctly rounded division, which ptxas builds from a reciprocal estimate plus refinement, with a subroutine (the four CALL.REL.NOINC) for the hard cases.

Where the renderer decides

Here is the codegen tail that does the work. It runs after final symbolic and before the renderer-specific final rewrite:

sink = graph_rewrite(sink, pm_cast_float_alu, name="cast float alu operands")
supported_ops = tuple(ren.code_for_op.keys())
pm_decomp = symbolic_simple+get_simplifying_rewrite_patterns(supported_ops)
sink = graph_rewrite(sink, pm_decomp, name="early decompositions")
sink = graph_rewrite(sink, pm_dtype_decomps+pm_commit_weak, ctx=(set(), ren), name="decomp dtypes")
pm_decomp = pm_decomp+\
  get_late_rewrite_patterns(supported_ops, bool(DISABLE_FAST_IDIV))+\
  get_transcendental_patterns(supported_ops, TRANSCENDENTAL>=2)
sink = graph_rewrite(sink, pm_decomp, ctx=ren, name="late decompositions")

That is to . The three pattern builders are each cached on the tuple of supported ops, so a renderer gets its own fixed set of rewrites.

  • get_simplifying_rewrite_patterns : Python's floor division and floor modulo become C's truncating CDIV/CMOD, powers of two become shifts and masks, MAX becomes compare + where if the target lacks it.
  • get_late_rewrite_patterns : multiply by 2^k becomes a shift, optional magic-number division, MULACC fusion for targets that list it.
  • get_transcendental_patterns :
for op,f in ((Ops.EXP2, xexp2), (Ops.LOG2, xlog2), (Ops.SIN, xsin)):
  if op not in ops or force_transcendental:
    pat += [(UPat(op, dtype=TRANSCENDENTAL_DTYPES, src=(UPat.var("d"),)), f), ...]
if Ops.SQRT not in ops or force_transcendental: pat.append((UPat(Ops.SQRT, ...), lambda d: xpow(d, d.const_like(0.5))))

So there are two reasons a software transcendental gets inlined: the renderer has no native version, or TRANSCENDENTAL is at least 2 (the default is 1, ). The CUDA C renderer has all of them: exp2(x), log2(x), sin(x), sqrt(x) and (1/x) come from the base C renderer, with hexp2/hsin/… overrides for half and bfloat16 . tinygrad's own PTX renderer also has all of them, but as the hardware approximations ex2.approx, lg2.approx, sin.approx, sqrt.approx, rcp.approx . tinygrad's CPU LLVM IR renderer has no math library to call and no EXP2, LOG2 or SIN in its code_for_op , so it gets the software versions from the generic get_transcendental_patterns above. (The xexp2/xlog2 import in is only used by AMDLLVMRenderer, for float64, .)

The decision matters less than it looks for CUDA C, because "native" there means "call the CUDA math library". The C function sin is not an instruction; it is libdevice's sinf, a few hundred instructions of its own. The next sections compare what each choice costs on the L4.

exp: the default kernel

With the default settings, a.exp() on a 1024×1024 float32 tensor gives one kernel. The MUL by 1.4426950216293335 came from the frontend; EXP2 rendered as the C call exp2:

Switch to the PTX and SASS tabs. NVRTC compiled exp2f to ex2.approx.f32, a single PTX instruction, and ptxas turned each of those into four SASS instructions:

FSETP.GEU.AND P0, PT, R7, -126, PT ;
@!P0 FMUL R7, R7, 0.5 ;
MUFU.EX2 R11, R7 ;
@!P0 FMUL R11, R11, R11 ;

MUFU.EX2 is the hardware's special-function unit: one 2^x per lane, but it flushes results below 2^−126 (the denormal range) to zero. The non-flushing ex2.approx.f32 must keep them, so ptxas guards it: if the argument is below −126, halve it, compute 2^(d/2), and square the result. This is the whole cost of the default exp: 4 FMUL for the scale, 4 compare, 8 predicated FMUL, 4 MUFU. The SASS has 33 instructions (not counting padding NOPs) and uses 16 registers.

exp in software: xexp2, step by step

Setting TRANSCENDENTAL=2 makes get_transcendental_patterns replace EXP2 with xexp2 even though CUDA has it. It is a SLEEF-style implementation targeting 1 ulp, built entirely from UOps. The algorithm is the classic one, and every step is visible in the captured kernel:

  1. Mask specials. _lazy_map_numbers replaces ±inf and NaN with 0 so the arithmetic below stays finite; they are patched back at the end. In the C source these are the alu2..alu5 selects.
  2. Range reduction. q = rintk(x) rounds half away from zero by adding ±0.5 and truncating ; s = x − q is in [−½, ½].
  3. Polynomial. polyN(s, [...]) with 7 float32 coefficients . polyN is a Horner fold, acc*x + c for each coefficient .
  4. Scale by 2^q. ldexp2k(u, q) multiplies by pow2if(q>>1) and pow2if(q − q>>1) . pow2if(n) is a bit trick: (n + 127) << 23, bitcast to float, is exactly 2^n .
  5. Clamp. d ≥ 128 gives +inf, d < −150 gives 0, NaN gives NaN.

Move the slider. The figure runs these exact steps in float32 with fused multiply-adds, as the kernel does, and reports the error against a float64 exp. Press "animate" to walk through the steps and watch the exponent field get built by a shift.

CPU experiment, to check the figure. On DEV=CPU with TRANSCENDENTAL=2, tinygrad's own Tensor([...]).exp2() for x = −149.5, −126.3, −3.7, −0.5, 0, 0.25, 1, 2.5, 10.3, 63.9, 100.1, 127.9 returned the same twelve float32 values, bit for bit, as this page's JavaScript emulation.

Why split q in two? For q = 128 a single pow2if(128) would need an exponent field of 255, which means infinity. Splitting into 64 + 64 keeps both factors normal, and the product u·2^64·2^64 still rounds correctly to the largest floats. The same split makes results near 2^−149 (denormals) come out right: 2^−75 · 2^−75 multiplied by u underflows gracefully instead of producing a garbage exponent.

A few things to read in it:

  • The polynomial is the nested ((((0.00015359208919107914f*alu29)+0.0013392627006396651f)*alu29)+… expression. In SASS it is six FFMA per element: ptxas contracted each multiply-add pair into one fused instruction (NVRTC contracts by default). The first coefficient lives in a register (0x39210d90 is 0.00015359209f) and the rest are immediates.
  • tg_bitcast<float>((int)(((alu25+127)<<23))) is pow2if. In SASS it is one instruction, LEA R10, R5, 0x3f800000, 0x17: shift R5 left by 23 and add 0x3f800000, which is 127<<23 (the bit pattern of 1.0f). The tg_bitcast template from the kernel prefix compiles to nothing.
  • ptxas turned the d ≥ 128 ? inf : … select into a real branch, the BSSY/@P0 BRA/BSYNC blocks: when the answer is inf, the polynomial is skipped. That is why there are 5 BRA and 4 BSSY in a kernel whose C source has no if.

33 instructions became 151, and the four MUFU are gone. And yet:

The two kernels ran in 24.58 µs and 23.55 µs; the software version was not slower. Both read 4 MB and write 4 MB. At the L4's roughly 300 GB/s that is about 28 µs of DRAM traffic, more than the measured times, so part of the input must be served from the 48 MB L2 left warm between timing runs. Either way both kernels are limited by memory traffic, not math, and ~30 extra float instructions per element disappear into the shadow of the loads. The difference between the two times is run-to-run noise, not a speedup. Accuracy against numpy is identical too: the largest error is 1.52587890625e-05 for both, on outputs up to 92.8, where one float32 ulp is 7.6e-06. Both are within 2 ulp.

sin: the expensive one

Sine is harder than exp because its range reduction is harder. For exp, subtracting an integer is exact. For sine you must subtract a multiple of π, and π is irrational: x − q·π in float32 loses digits as q grows, and for x around 10^5 a naive reduction has only two or three correct digits left. Drag x below, switch between the two reduction schemes, and press "unwind" to watch x wrap around the circle.

tinygrad's xsin works like this:

  1. Mask ±inf/NaN to 0, take |x| and remember the sign.
  2. Cody–Waite reduction modulo π for small |x| : q = round(|x|/π), then r = |x| − q·π computed as four FMAs with π split into −3.1414794921875, −0.000113159…, −1.98e-9 and −1.2e-10 . The leading pieces have few mantissa bits, so q·piece is exact and the sum stays accurate. The error plot shows what this buys over a single float32 π.
  3. Payne–Hanek reduction for large |x| : multiply the mantissa by 190 bits of 2/π (two_over_pi_f) with 64-bit integer arithmetic, and read the quadrant from the top two bits.
  4. An odd degree-9 polynomial sin_poly (5 coefficients) on the reduced argument , with the sign set by the parity of q.

The catch is step 3. A GPU kernel cannot take one path for some lanes and another for others without paying for both, so xsin computes both reductions for every element and selects with (x_abs<switch_over).where(sin_poly_small(...), sin_poly_large(...)) . Only fast=True skips Payne–Hanek, and the pattern never passes it. Our inputs are standard normal, all far below the switch-over of 30, so all the Payne–Hanek work is thrown away.

The C source is 271 lines for four elements. Look at the highlighted divisions cast10/cast11: they are _shr_lazy, which shifts right by dividing by pow2if(y) cast to uint64 . The divisor is a runtime value, so nvcc cannot strength-reduce it, and a 64-bit integer division has no instruction on the GPU. In the SASS each one is a branch: if the divisor fits in 32 bits, an inline path (I2F.U32.RP, MUFU.RCP for a reciprocal estimate, one Newton step); otherwise a CALL.REL.NOINC 0x33e0 to the 64-bit division subroutine: 12 of each for 4 elements. ptxas needed 62 registers. (The captured SASS listing stops at offset 0x2ea0, so the subroutine at 0x33e0 is not shown and the instruction mix below covers the 747 instructions before the cut.)

The default kernel is different in kind. sin(x) in CUDA C is libdevice's sinf:

Read the first 20 instructions of the SASS and you will recognize the algorithm from the figure in quadrant mode: multiply by 2/π (FMUL R0, R8, 0.63661974668502807617), convert to an integer quadrant (F2I.NTZ), and reduce with three FFMAs by π/2 split as 1.5707962512969970703, 7.5497894158615963534e-08, 5.3903029534742383927e-15. Then FSETP.GE.AND P0, PT, |R8|, 105615, PT and @!P0 BRA: if |x| is below 105615 the whole Payne–Hanek part is jumped over. That slow path is a loop of LDG.E.CONSTANT over the __cudart_i2opi_f table (2/π in bytes, declared in the PTX), stores to a 32-byte local stack frame (STL), and a DMUL in double precision. Then it evaluates a sine or cosine polynomial, chosen per lane with FSEL on the low bit of the quadrant. There is no MUFU.SIN anywhere: libdevice computes sine with FFMAs. Only sin.approx.f32, which tinygrad's PTX renderer emits , uses the hardware sine unit (the book has no L4 capture of a PTX-renderer sine).

libdevice's sine is a branch, and every lane takes the short side, so its cost stays inside the memory time: 23.55 µs, about the same as exp. tinygrad's software sine is straight-line code with both reductions and the 64-bit divisions, and that is enough work to make the kernel compute-bound: 69.63 µs, about three times as long. Accuracy is fine in both: the largest errors are 5.96e-08 (default) and 1.19e-07 (software), one or two float32 ulps of 1.0.

The lesson for decompositions. On a GPU a where evaluates both sides. A decomposition that is "only taken for large inputs" in scalar code is paid for by every element unless the compiler can turn the select into a branch, as ptxas did for exp's inf case, or unless the code is written with a branch, as libdevice is.

Integer division and modulo

The third example is a // 7 + a % 10 on int32. Python semantics are floor division and floor modulo: -7 // 2 == -4 and -7 % 2 == 1. C rounds toward zero: -7 / 2 == -3, -7 % 2 == -1. The early decompositions translate one into the other :

def floordiv_to_idiv(a:UOp, b:UOp) -> UOp:
  if (a.vmin >= 0 and b.vmin > 0) or (a.vmax <= 0 and b.vmax < 0): return a.alu(Ops.CDIV, b)
  return a.alu(Ops.CDIV, b) - (a.alu(Ops.CMOD, b).ne(0) & (a<0).ne(b<0))

If the symbolic range of a proves it non-negative, floor and truncation agree and a plain / is emitted. Our inputs are in [0, 2^20), but tinygrad doesn't know that: a value loaded from an int32 buffer can be anything in the int32 range. So the kernel carries the correction terms, for both the division and the modulo (, r + (r≠0 and a<0 ? 10 : 0)). Step through the passes to see the FLOORDIV disappear in early decompositions:

The source still says val0.x/7 and val0.x%10. tinygrad's own magic-number division (fast_idiv) did not run, for two separate reasons:

  • DISABLE_FAST_IDIV defaults to 1, with the comment # TODO: this is broken for some indexing .
  • Even when enabled, it only fires on values known to be non-negative .

On CUDA it does not matter, because NVVM does the same strength reduction. In the PTX tab, /7 became mul.hi.s32 %r54, %r6, -1840700269 followed by add, shifts and a sign fix-up; %10 became mul.hi.s32 …, 1717986919, then x − 10·q. In SASS those are IMAD.HI R16, R4, -0x6db6db6d, R2 and IMAD.HI R9, R5, 0x66666667, RZ. 0x66666667 is ⌈2^34/10⌉, and −0x6db6db6d is 0x92492493 = ⌈2^34/7⌉ read as a signed int. There is no division instruction in the kernel; the 92 instructions for 4 elements are multiplies, shifts, adds and selects, and the kernel ran in 22.53 µs, the same memory-bound time as the float kernels.

tinygrad's magicgu computes the same kind of constant, adapted from Hacker's Delight chapter 10 , and like that version it takes the largest possible dividend vmax as an input, so a small known range gets a small magic number that fits in 32 bits. Try it:

A CPU experiment shows what happens when tinygrad can prove the range. Rendering CUDA C (with tinygrad's NVCCRenderer, which prints the same C as the CUDA renderer) for a 4096-element int32 tensor gave:

// (a & 1023) // 7, DISABLE_FAST_IDIV=1 (default)
make_int4(((val0.x&1023)/7), ...
// (a & 1023) // 7, DISABLE_FAST_IDIV=0: vmax is 1023, magicgu(1023, 7) = (1171, 13)
make_int4((((val0.x&1023)*1171)>>13), ...
// a // 8 and a % 8: powers of two never need magic numbers
make_int4((val0.x>>3), ...        make_int4((val0.x&7), ...

The last line is the rule at and : in two's complement, an arithmetic shift right by k is floor division by 2^k and a mask is floor modulo, for negative numbers too. So power-of-two division gets no correction terms at all, which matters a lot for index arithmetic, where every // and % from a reshape is by a shape that is very often a power of two.

Smaller decompositions you have already seen

MAX. The C renderers have no MAX in code_for_op, so (UPat(Ops.MAX), lambda m: (m.src[0] < m.src[1]).where(m.src[1], m.src[0])) fires . The max_cols kernel from chapter 5 shows it as ((val1<val0)?val0:val1):

ptxas does not always turn this back into a hardware max. Its SASS has 78 FSETP + 78 FSEL pairs and only 2 FMNMX. The reason is NaN: (a<b)?b:a returns a whenever either operand is NaN (so it propagates a NaN in a and drops one in b), while FMNMX returns the non-NaN operand, so it can only be used where the compiler can rule NaN out. The PTX renderer lists MAX and emits max.f32 directly.

relu. relu is (self > 0).where(self, 0) by design, not maximum(x, 0) ; the comment says the maximum version would pass half the gradient at 0. In the fused_chain kernel it renders as ((0.0f<alu1)?alu1:0.0f), and here ptxas did produce 4 FMNMX: comparing with the constant 0 is NaN-safe in the right way.

Multiplication by a power of two becomes a shift for integers (x * 2^k → x << k, ). That is where every (gidx0<<7)+(lidx0<<2) in this book's kernels comes from.

Half-precision transcendentals are not polynomial-expanded in half: exp casts to float32 first , and for float types outside float16/32/64 (bfloat16, fp8) the transcendental patterns cast to float32, apply the op, and cast back .

PyTorch: libdevice all the way

PyTorch never decomposes these itself. Eager mode calls ATen's exp_kernel_cuda and sin_kernel_cuda (templated vectorized_elementwise_kernel<4, …>), compiled ahead of time with nvcc. Inductor emits Triton with tl_math/libdevice calls, and Triton links NVIDIA's libdevice bitcode, so the math is the same library tinygrad's default CUDA C path ends up in.

In the TTIR, exp is tt.extern_elementwise … symbol = "__nv_expf". libdevice's expf is itself a decomposition, and a familiar one. In the SASS, per element:

  • FFMA.FTZ R4, R2, R6, 0.5 and FFMA.FTZ.RM R4, R4, R7, 12582913 round x·log₂e to an integer using the "add 1.5·2^23" trick.
  • SHF.L.U32 R4, R4, 0x17 shifts that integer into the exponent field: tinygrad's pow2if again.
  • Two FFMAs (with 1.4426950216293334961 and the low-order part 1.925963033500011079e-08) form the fraction, and MUFU.EX2 computes 2^fraction.
  • One FMUL combines them.

So libdevice uses the hardware unit for the small part only, where it is accurate, and does the range reduction in software. The .FTZ flags come from Triton compiling with flush-to-zero. Triton compiled this kernel twice (pick the copy in the selector): the first copy, shown by default, loads two floats per thread (LDG.E.64); the second loads eight with two LDG.E.128, where tinygrad loads four with one.

Sine is math.sin in TTIR, which lowers to __nv_sinf: the same 2/π multiply, the same 105615 threshold, the same local-memory Payne–Hanek fallback, and no MUFU.SIN. The only difference from tinygrad's default kernel is FTZ and the vector width.

The PyTorch captures report 5.09 µs for Inductor's exp and 5.79 µs for its sine. Those are torch.profiler kernel times, measured differently from tinygrad's CUDA-event timing, and 8 MB in 5 µs is faster than the L4's DRAM, which suggests the data was still in its 48 MB L2 cache. Compare the instruction streams across frameworks, not these absolute times.

Summary

  • The frontend reduces every math method to EXP2, LOG2, SIN, SQRT, RECIPROCAL plus ALU ops. Late in codegen, each renderer's code_for_op keys decide which of them stay.
  • CUDA C keeps all of them and delegates to the CUDA math library: exp2 → ex2.approx → MUFU.EX2 with a denormal guard; sin → libdevice sinf with FFMA polynomials and a branched Payne–Hanek path, no MUFU.SIN.
  • TRANSCENDENTAL=2 forces tinygrad's SLEEF-derived polynomials. Software exp (range reduction, 7-term Horner, exponent built by (q+127)<<23) costs nothing in a memory-bound kernel: 23.55 vs 24.58 µs. Software sin computes both reductions for every element, including 64-bit divisions, and takes 69.63 vs 23.55 µs.
  • Floor division and modulo become truncating C operators plus correction terms unless the range is known to be non-negative; powers of two become shifts and masks. Magic-number division is off by default in tinygrad, and NVVM does it anyway (IMAD.HI with 0x66666667 and 0x92492493).
  • MAX becomes compare + select on CUDA C; ptxas recovers FMNMX only where NaN semantics allow.
